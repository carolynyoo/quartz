## Summary (incl. main characters, stakes, setting)
_American Hagwon_ centers around the Koh family over 15 years. They move from Seoul to Sydney to Orange County, CA as they navigate hardships including a comically villanious harlot, the IMF crisis, and other tragedies. Many other surrounding characters are introduced from their families, friend groups, hagwon, and church community. The book examines the hierarchical importance of education in Korean culture and the role that hagwons play, as it interplays with capitalism to take advantage of this societal value. 

## How I discovered it (rec, why now)
Loved Min Jin Lee's past two novels, so I preordered _American Hagwon_.

## How I felt (thoughts on writing, themes, plot, pacing)
Upon starting the book, I quickly found the amount of characters frustrating. The flow of new people introduced was constant, and very few of them were recurring or important. 
I would have been willing to forgive this, and eventually got over it 1/3 of the way into the book, as Lee started to center around the core cast of characters living in Orange County. However the entire Koh family and Fortress/Exodus hagwon cast are too perfect and one note! Why couldn't some of the dissenting opinions regarding education play out within the family? Why couldn't Mido, who pushes off finishing her degree for the sake of her family, experience more actual repercussion or shame for this decision? 
Things were touched upon here and there but there were such little stakes for the Koh family beyond Danbi the antagonist. They had rich connections, such as their aunt Sonya or Peter's mom Renee, to save them financially if they were in hardship—and never were these connections exhausted or fed up with helping them. 
I was worried this book would feel slow, but given my personal interest in my own culture and the OC setting, I sped through this novel after the first third. I don't mind Min Jin Lee's straightforward writing style—BUT it was deeply irritating to me how repetitive the book is, explaining motivations or plot points that were covered not that long ago. I've seen mentions that Lee is influenced by Victorian lit, which is also long and repetitive because of its serialization, but in a modern book this choice comes across as very preachy and undermines the reader. 
Overall I'm a bit bummed with this ride. There was a lot to enjoy about this book, with so much potential—the deep research, the real felt sense of Orange County and Seoul with specificity in descriptions, the hard working nature and sincere heart of a lot of these characters. I wish there were more moral gray within the Koh family and within Fortress/Exodus. It would have been exciting  to explore more unspoken church social etiquette—those chapters of Danbi navigating church waters were genuinely so fun. I respect Min Jin Lee's willingness to capture the Korean American diaspora and put forth a parable to question the hierarchy of education, but I wish she didn't have to do that at the sacrifice of writing an actually interesting novel. 

## Who would like it
Those who want more insight into Korean diaspora's relationship with education, and secondarily church community
Those who enjoy the flatness/villainry of K-drama characters and seeing their downfall (lol)

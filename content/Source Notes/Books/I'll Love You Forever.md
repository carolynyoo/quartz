## Summary in 3 sentences

1. Memoir in essays on #kpop
2. Interweaving cultural criticism with personal story of upbringing in SFV and embedding within conservative Christian culture

## How I discovered it

I picked this book up because I know this author. She came to the book club for #Asian women that I used to run in Manhattan in 2018-2019, and met one of her best friends there. I always tried to be friendly toward her but there was a distance she kept, I think because she felt insecure or jealous or made assumptions about me and my happiness. I’m not sure. Whatever the case, she would occasionally come up in conversation between me and my sister or my other good friend who I met through the same book club because of her Instagram. This author is a chronic oversharer, a quality that I both admire and feel repelled by, perhaps even shame on her behalf for repeating the same insecurities and strong opinions over and over. I admire the strongly held opinions while also finding it myopic and frustrating that they are so incendiary, as to make up for her insecurities (of which she has many).

## Who should read it?

Fans of K-pop and #Korean culture

## How it changed me

_Behavior, ideas, perspectives, emotional shifts_

This book is a memoir in essays on K-pop, and no matter how it is written, I’m glad it exists as one of the first cultural criticism books on K-pop interweaving personal narrative. I see myself in glimpses of her story—we grew up in the same San Fernando Valley, though I was more tangential in church community while she was deeply embedded. She writes about herself, her community, and her family with compassion and nuance that I appreciate, despite its utter repetition. This book could’ve benefited from being shorter in this regard, or have more scenes perhaps. I also wish she dived further into her relationship with sexuality here as she previously has in a Buzzfeed essay. While I understand it’s painful (I mean, here I am having abandoned my own memoir) it would also have made for better story. As it reads now, much of her reaching toward K-pop is a distraction and emollient for the deep shame she carries around her body and worth. The insecurity drips off the pages but I get the sense that the story is not complete, though she has more friendships, more community, and she has writing.

The cultural criticism portion of this book was weak. Much of the book is comprised of rants that lose the reader. I am confident readers will still learn _something_ from it, unless they are deeply studied in Korean culture and the K-pop world. But I wish the research were both more thorough and concise, and better structured. The book’s structure failed the most. Though the conceit of having a K-pop idol for each chapter was a good one, it did not adhere to a specific topic per each (which would have made it stronger). Some could absolutely have been cut. For example the entirety of her reflections on BTS was, in my opinion, only included for marketing purposes. They were weak and she did not have much of a relationship to this group, only Suga perhaps. And by relationship I don’t mean her specific thoughts on who she thinks idols are or why she likes them. That is not literature I care to read—I can easily find million versions of that on social media.

**Through this book I’ve been able to reflect a bit on my own relationship with K-pop**, which started casually through my sister’s love for 1st gen idols and daily summer vacations in Korea, to earnest admiration and love for BoA, looking down upon it with disdain for 2nd gen, then true fandom for SHINee and EXO during college when I felt my loneliest. K-pop was a flotation device, a way to connect with others through an impartial third party through which us fans could map our desires and feelings without needing to have the self-remonstrative thoughts of who we were and how far we were from who we wanted to be.

I gravitated toward K-pop when I could finally be on the internet freely with my own laptop. My mom and sister wondered curiously how I could spend so much time on the internet and what I could possibly be doing. I was exacting my control in a digital realm, organizing photos of my bias Key into neat folders, translating interviews and moderating a Livejournal K-pop news community. This was what I could do when I felt I was powerless to leave the house or exert any color of myself in a home where I felt perpetually unsafe to express my emotions, and thus myself.
